The Longest Night of Techno at ADE Turns 21 the Year Amsterdam Dance Event Turns 30

Dave Clarke Presents returns to Melkweg 23 Oct 2026 for its 21st ADE edition — 18 artists across 4 rooms closing out ADE's 30th anniversary.

Melkweg on Lijnbaansgracht, Amsterdam — home of Dave Clarke Presents for most of its 21-edition run. Photo via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.

Three decades of Amsterdam Dance Event have produced many rituals — panels, showcases, boat parties, warehouse stunts. Only one has never missed a beat in the last 21 of them. On Friday 23 October 2026, from 23:00, Dave Clarke Presents takes over four rooms of Melkweg — Max, Oz, Up and Cinema — for its 21st consecutive edition, and its first as the anchor of ADE’s 30th anniversary programme. Eighteen artists, four back-to-backs, one film strand. No headliner slot, no reunion tour, no anniversary encore. Just the party that has quietly become the longest-running fixture in the world’s largest club-music festival.

What ADE is, and where Dave Clarke Presents sits inside it

ADE was born in 1996 out of a Buma/Stemra-backed idea to give Dutch music industry professionals a place to meet. The first edition ran across a single hotel on Vijzelgracht and drew just over 100 people over a weekend. Thirty years on, the Amsterdam Dance Event is a five-day city takeover: the 2025 edition counted 600,000 visitors from more than 140 countries, 3,400 artists, and 1,200 events across roughly 300 venues — figures that made it, by every measurable definition, the largest club festival on the planet. The 30th edition runs 21–25 October 2026.

Dave Clarke Presents joined the programme in 2004, at Club 11, before quickly finding its home at Melkweg. Twenty-one editions later — one for every ADE since — it is the festival’s longest-running individual event. “A cornerstone of Amsterdam Dance Event for 21 editions” is how Melkweg’s own copy for the 2026 edition puts it. Nothing else on the ADE calendar can make that claim.

Why this edition matters

Anniversaries are the easiest thing in electronic music to fake: throw a legend on the top line, sell it as a nostalgia trip, cash the ticket. This is not that. The 2026 edition of Dave Clarke Presents is billed with unusual bluntness — “no filler, no nostalgia act — just a relentless celebration of the culture.” The programming choices back that up. Three of the four b2bs pair a foundational act with a producer twenty or thirty years younger. There are five women in the top half of the lineup. The oldest performer on the bill (Egyptian Lover, 63, born 31 August 1963) shares billing with names who put out their first EPs in 2023.

The other signal is architectural. Previous Dave Clarke Presents editions have typically used Melkweg’s Max — the 1,500-capacity main hall, opened 1995 and expanded in 2007 — as their spine. The 2026 edition spreads across four rooms simultaneously: Max, Oz, Up and Cinema, with a curated late-night film strand running in parallel to the dancefloor. This is the closest Dave Clarke Presents has ever come to functioning as a festival-within-a-festival.

The lineup, decoded

The 18-artist bill breaks cleanly into three currents, and reading it as a single flat list misses the point.

The pioneers. The night’s centre of gravity sits with a generation of artists who were shaping electronic music before ADE existed. Dave Clarke himself — born Brighton, 19 September 1968, Amsterdam resident since 2008, given the moniker “Baron of Techno” by John Peel after the Red 1/2/3 12”s of 1994–95 — headlines his own party for the 21st time.

Dave Clarke. Portrait via Wikimedia Commons — license to be confirmed on file page before publish.

Luke Slater, British techno’s most consistently mutating figure since his first release in 1989 and the mind behind Planetary Assault Systems, goes b2b with Rene Wise, a UK producer half his age; on paper this is the most interesting pairing of the night. DJ Hell, founder of Munich’s International DeeJay Gigolo Records — established in 1996, the same year as ADE — and the label widely credited as the germ cell of electroclash, plays a solo set. Egyptian Lover — Gregory Broussard, Los Angeles, the man who put the Roland TR-808 into West Coast rap on 1984’s “Egypt, Egypt” — connects the roots of dance music to everything downstream. DJ Stingray 313 (Sherard Ingram, Detroit; touring DJ for Drexciya from the late ’80s until James Stinson’s death in September 2002) goes b2b with Berlin’s Mell G. Dasha Rush, the Moscow-born, Berlin-based head of Fullpanda Records, brings the night’s most experimental sensibility. Veronica Vasicka, who founded New York’s Minimal Wave Records in 2005 and effectively named the genre it collects, plays her first Dave Clarke Presents.

The current wave. Daniel Avery goes b2b with Richard Fearless — the pair have been running their Goo all-night parties in London and beyond, and their 2025 Lot Radio set is the reference point for what to expect here. DJ Godfather, the Detroit ghettotech mainstay, goes b2b with Von Di. Parrish Smith brings his industrial-leaning EBM.

The new generation. Japan’s Risa Taniguchi — No. 1 on Beatport’s Techno chart with the So Loud EP, now on Chris Liebing’s CLR — Berlin-based Nastia Reigel, whose Rosa Damask alias explores darker post-punk terrain and appears separately on the bill as a live act, plus Lizz V, Brutuzz, Mell G, Von Di, Rene Wise, L.F.T. (live), Ash Code (live) and Dirk Wiertz (live) round out a bill that is unusually top-heavy on live sets for a Dave Clarke party. Five live acts across one night is a deliberate curatorial signal about where the party thinks the genre is heading.

A primer, if you’re new to this

You don’t need this section if the names on that lineup already ring bells. If they don’t, here’s a ninety-minute route through what they mean: Egyptian Lover’s 1984 electro, the Detroit orbit that shaped Stingray, the minimal-wave archive Vasicka spent a decade rebuilding, the European ’90s of Slater and Hell and Clarke, and the new generation working now. In order, one listen. You’ll know by the end whether Melkweg on 23 October is a night for you or one to skip.

Why count this night

Every ADE produces one or two events that historians will look back on. Dave Clarke Presents has been that event, quietly, for two decades — not because any single edition was legendary, but because the party has kept its editorial line intact while every other techno institution around it drifted, sold out, or dissolved. The 30th ADE is the moment where the two anniversaries meet: 30 years of the festival, 21 years of the same party inside it. It is also, plausibly, the last edition where you will hear Egyptian Lover, Luke Slater, Veronica Vasicka and Dave Clarke on the same night in the same building. That is not marketing; that is the actuarial reality of pioneer techno in the late 2020s. The line about “no nostalgia act” is aimed at the programming. It is not a comment on what the room will feel like at 04:00.
